Why DFS-Reorder falls behind Sparse GE and Dense GE A reading of decoder_comparison_report.html, with the fill-in numbers that report does not plot. The theoretical claim — reordering should make Gaussian elimination faster — is true in general. It is not true for the residuals this decoder actually solves.

BEC / erasure decoding Sparse GF(2) GE Fill-in vs reorder cost Simpler decoder

Open decoder_comparison_report.html and the red Sparse + DFS curve does not pull away from green Sparse GE. On wall-clock it is indistinguishable from both Sparse and Dense. That is not a measurement glitch. DFS is paying a reorder tax that the elimination never earns back, and on the 2-core left after peeling it often increases fill-in rather than reducing it. The simpler BEC decoder that the numbers support is plain peeling, then one Sparse GE in natural column order — no DFS, no VH, no cluster tree.

1 · What the comparison report actually ran

Key idea
decoder_comparison_report.html is the full paper cascade (M=0 peeling → M=1 fully-erased generators → M=2 VH products → cluster decoder). The three conditions swap only the Gaussian-elimination backend inside the leftover classical clusters. They are not three different decoders. They are three solvers for the same tiny leftover systems.

2 · How to read the four charts

Key idea
The report splits cost on purpose. GE time is elimination only; DFS construction lives in DFS reorder time; mean decode time is the number a user actually pays. Adding GE + reorder is the honest cost of “Sparse + DFS” as a solver. Decode time is the honest cost of the decoder.

Heads-up — DFS is not slower than Dense on this report. It is a wash.
  • Failure rate. The three curves coincide (DFS picks a different particular solution on a handful of trials: 0.047 vs 0.048).
  • GE / elimination only. DFS is a ~6% win over Sparse (9.49 vs 10.07 ms). That is real, and tiny.
  • Reorder. Building the DFS order costs 8.58 ms — almost the entire Sparse GE bill, and ~15× the 0.58 ms it saved.
  • Honest solver cost (GE + reorder). DFS is 1.8× slower than Sparse and still 2.6× faster than Dense. This is the chart the report does not draw, and it is where DFS “falls behind Sparse”.
  • Mean decode time. All three sit at ~36 ms. GE is 10 µs per trial on average (10.07 ms / 1000). That is 0.03% of the decode. Peeling and cluster-graph bookkeeping dominate. No GE trick can move this plot, which is why DFS also cannot “beat Dense” on the decoder the user sees.

Toric3 is the same story at toy scale: 148 GE calls, Sparse elimination 1.24 ms, DFS elimination 1.13 ms, DFS reorder 1.19 ms, decode ~0.33 ms/trial for all three. The 18-qubit code is too small for fill-in to exist.

3 · Why theory said DFS should win

Key idea
Sparse GE costs roughly the number of 1s it touches, not the matrix area. Each pivot XOR rows[q] ^= pivot_set can add 1s to row q (fill-in). A good pivot order keeps those XORs local so row-sets stay small. DFS on the column-intersection graph is the cheap heuristic that tries to walk locally: next pivot = a column that shares a check with the current one.
Pen & paper — a chain, where DFS really is peeling
Residual c0 — chk — c1 — chk — c2 — chk — c3 (a tree):
DFS order (c0,c1,c2,c3): each pivot meets one neighbour -> ~zero fill-in Random / hub-first order: one pivot links far-apart rows -> a dense block forms
This picture is correct — and it is also exactly the structure that M=0 peeling already deleted for free before GE is ever called. What remains for GE is the 2-core: every remaining check has at least two erased qubits. That is a graph of cycles, not a chain. DFS is not a fill-reducing algorithm for cyclic graphs (minimum-degree / AMD / nested dissection are).

Natural order already has HGP geometry: qubits are stored row-major from the Kronecker product, so consecutive indices are usually grid-neighbours. DFS is not starting from a random permutation; it is trying to beat an order that is already local.

4 · Four reasons it does not

Reason 1 — GE is the wrong bottleneck in this report

On C_625 the cascade spends ~36 ms/trial in peeling, VH, and cluster-graph updates, and ~10 µs in GE. Sparse already beat Dense 4.7× on GE (47.55 ms vs 10.07 ms over the whole 1000-trial bin). That win is invisible in decode time. DFS cannot beat Dense on a plot Dense is not even on, and cannot beat Sparse on a plot where GE is 0.03% of the y-axis.

Reason 2 — the leftover matrix is 15×20

Fill-reducing orderings amortize their construction cost over a large sparse factorisation. Here the factorisation is a classical (3,4)-LDPC chunk with ≤20 columns. Peak row weight is bounded by 20. The 0.58 ms DFS saved in elimination is smaller than the 8.58 ms spent building the column graph by scanning dense NumPy columns:

H_col = H[:, c]
for r in range(num_rows):     # full height of H, per erased column
    if H_col[r]:
        col_to_rows[c].add(r)

For a 15×20 cluster this scan is cheap in absolute terms and still comparable to the 34 µs elimination. For a global Hz (next section) it becomes a large tax.

Reason 3 — after peeling, DFS is the wrong heuristic

Peeling removed the degree-1 vertices. The residual is a 2-core (cycles). DFS on a cyclic graph produces a long snaking path whose elimination front can be wider than natural index order. That is the opposite of nested dissection. The ordering-proof experiment (below) measures this directly: at high erasure DFS increases XOR-writes and peak row size versus natural order. Even minimum-degree, which does cut fill-in, still loses on wall-clock because computing the order is more expensive than the fill it saves.

Reason 4 — a different particular solution is not a better decoder

Changing pivot order changes which columns are free. The solver still returns a solution of H·e = s with free variables set to 0, but not necessarily the same vector, and not necessarily the minimum Hamming weight vector in the coset. That is why the failure-rate curves wiggle by a few trials (C_625: 47 vs 48 failures at p=0.32; C_1600 peel→GE at p=0.45: DFS 0.300 vs Sparse 0.3004). DFS is not a decoding improvement; it is a different coset representative.

5 · Direct fill-in evidence (sister experiments)

Key idea
The comparison report cannot test the fill-in hypothesis: the systems are too small, and it never recorded XOR-writes. Two later campaigns do: they skip VH and the cluster tree, peel once (M=0), then feed the whole residual to one Sparse GE. That is the setting where reordering was supposed to shine.

Heads-up — even a fill-reducing order loses
Minimum-degree is the textbook heuristic, and it does what it says: 14% fewer XOR-writes, smaller peak row, slightly faster elimination. Computing it (clique-fill updates on the column graph) costs 11 s, ~28× the 0.4 s it saved. DFS is worse than natural on fill-in and still pays a 5 s tax. No column ordering we tried is a net win for this residual.

6 · The simpler BEC decoder the numbers point to

Key idea
For the binary erasure channel the decoder already knows the support. The job is “solve Hz·e = s on the erased columns.” Peeling does the tree part in linear time. One Sparse GE in natural order does the rest. VH, the cluster tree, and DFS are extra machinery that do not earn their keep on the codes and rates measured here.

Pen & paper — why the cascade dies at high erasure
The cluster decoder refuses a cluster that still contains a cycle. At p=0.45 the residual is almost surely one giant cyclic 2-core, so the cascade records 1 GE call in 2500 trials and a failure rate of 0.9996. Peeling→GE does not refuse: it hands that 2-core to Sparse GE and recovers 70% of the shots. That is the BEC-relevant decoder. DFS is an optional permutation in front of that GE, and the permutation loses.

7 · Takeaway

Key idea
Reordering improves GE when (i) the matrix is large, (ii) natural order is a bad permutation, and (iii) the heuristic actually cuts fill-in by more than it costs to compute. None of the three hold here.
  1. In decoder_comparison_report.html, GE runs on ≤20-column classical leftovers after a cascade that already ate the trees. DFS saves ~6% of an already-tiny elimination and spends that saving 15 times over on the reorder. Decode time does not move; Dense, Sparse, and DFS sit on top of each other because GE is 0.03% of the decoder.
  2. When GE is the residual (peeling→one global solve), DFS still loses: at p=0.45 it increases fill-in, slows elimination, and makes the decoder 1.31× slower than natural-order Sparse GE.
  3. Minimum-degree does cut fill-in and still loses on wall-clock. Column reordering is the wrong lever for this GF(2) residual in this Python set-XOR solver.
  4. The simpler effective BEC decoder is M=0 peeling + Sparse GE, natural column order. It is faster than the paper cascade at moderate erasure and is the only one of the two that still decodes once the 2-core percolates.
